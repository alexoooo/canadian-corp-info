# T3 investment distributions handling for corporations

**Who this is for**: owners of Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) who get a T3 from stock or ETF investments.  
If you have a corporate investment account (e.g. RBC Direct Investing), and you hold an ETF (e.g. XEI), you will need to account for the T3 tax slip.  

This document covers T3 slips issued by Canadian ETFs structured as a corporation or trust; mutual funds / real property / other asset types might work differently.  
There are other T slips to consider, such as T5 and T5008, which have their own tax treatment (outside the scope of this document).  
This document is aimed at small business corporations, other tax situations are out of scope (e.g. when revenue is over $500k).  
Using a Dividend Reinvestment Plan (DRIP) increases paperwork and complexity, this document assumes DRIP is not used.  
Dates below are given assuming your company has a year-end of December 31.  
Tax information can change over time (e.g. the capital gains inclusion rate was going to increase to 2/3, before the proposal was cancelled), the below is my understanding as of 2026.  


# T3 boxes

When you have a corporate investment account holding an ETF which pays distributions, you will see the following:
- Box 49: Actual amount of eligible dividends
- Box 21: Capital gains distribution
- Box 25: Foreign non-business income
- Box 34: Foreign non-business income tax paid
- Box 42: Amount resulting in cost base adjustment, typically Return of Capital (ROC)
- Box 26: Other income


# Matching ledger vs brokerage account

Reconciling your numbers is easier when you can match the numbers in your ledger to what you see in your bank/brokerage statements.  
You will want to match the cash amount in your investment account, as well as the book cost of your investments.  
Matching exactly is not always possible, but typically you can achieve this by carefully managing your ledger accounts.  

Book Cost (Book Value): amount shown on your investment statements, it typically includes your initial investment, increases when you buy, and decreases when you sell.  
Adjusted Cost Base (ACB): official tax term used by the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) to calculate capital gains or losses when you sell an asset.  
Ideally these two would be identical, but in practice you might see brokerage-dependent differences with the following:
- Phantom distributions (non-cash portion of Box 21 from ETF where no new units are issued), and cost base adjustments (box 42, typically ROC), and re-invested distributions.  
  Some brokers such as RBC Direct Investing do handle these, but possibly delayed until April, and in some cases missing entirely.
  Other brokers may not handle some or all of these at all, the behaviour can be inconsistent.  
- External holdings (same security in different account) and superficial loss rules may not be correctly tracked by your brokerage.  
All brokerages might occasionally forget to properly adjust the Book Cost  (e.g. with RBC if you sell the security before April) and sometimes report the wrong amount (which can be resolved by raising the issue with the brokerage), and they might even not update the ACB at all (ideally you should avoid these because it makes your life harder).  
For this reason, you will need to independently track the ACB in a spreadsheet to properly report taxes and also to validate what you see from your brokerage.  

Investment distributions will arrive in your corporate investment account throughout the year, but we won't know the final tax classification until we get the T3 (note that sometimes the statement will call them "Dividends", but that doesn't mean that they are actually eligible dividends).  
You will typically receive a T3 in the 2nd half of March, and you might need a call to your brokerage to get them to email the T3 (and other) investment package PDFs.  
The numbers you see from your brokerage are not final, they can retroactively change with the handling of T3 slips and other adjustments.  
You can match the numbers during the year, but then when you incorporate the T3 information this will change your numbers for the past year, and you will need a temporary plug to synchronize with the brokerage reporting.

If you are in doubt about the true tax treatment, or you doubt what is in your T3, you can calculate the expected values based on the PDF provided by the Canadian Tax Breakdown Reporting Service from CDS:
https://ctbsext.posttrade.cds.ca/ctbsExt/external-landing

# Preliminary tracking throughout the year

Until we know what the real classification is, the distributions can be tracked in a temporary location:  
Revenue (GIFI 8299) / Investment revenue (GIFI 8090) / Interest from other Canadian sources (GIFI 8094) / TBD investment distributions (code 8094-2)  
In March when the T3 arrives, we go back and reclassify to the precise tax treatment (as an accounting convention, the prior entries can be edited without explicit reversal).  

In some cases the T3 that you receive will have slightly different amounts vs what is in your brokerage statements (typically by up to a few pennies and over time these differences should wash out).  
You need to report the amounts as they appear on the T3, not what is in your actual account, the difference can be tracked in:  
Revenue (GIFI 8299) / Investment revenue (code 8090-parent) / Investment revenue adjustment (GIFI 8090)  

Before retroactive adjustments, you should be able to match the "Deposits - investment" (1002-2) and "Brokerage" (2303-1) accounts to what you see in the brokerage website and statements at any time throughout the year.  
But after the retroactive adjustments are made, the amounts in your past year brokerage statements will be out of sync.  
This accounting convention is a pragmatic tradeoff, it is possible to maintain both views, but it would require additional complexity.  
Depending on the brokerage, the amount might be propagated to the past (you will see different amounts in the historical view vs the past statements, or it might just be reflected going forward).  

These are the things that are useful to track separately from your ledger in a spreadsheet:  
- Cost base: trades (buy/sell) and ROC / capital gains dividends  
- Distributions: actual cash vs what's in the T3, and tax treatment (TBD initially and eventually corresponding to T3 boxes above)
- Paying dividends requires using GRIP/ERDTOH/NERDTOH which is handled by T2 software
- Capital dividend account: running total of all capital gains and losses (including capital gains distributions), to be used for a tax-efficient capital dividend  


# Eligible dividends - Box 49

Debit: "Deposits - investment" (1002-2) within year, or "Dividends receivable" (1060) if declared in December but paid in Jan.  
Credit: "Dividends from Canadian sources" (GIFI 8096).  

Schedule 3 (S3 - Dividends Received, Taxable Dividends Paid, and Part IV Tax Calculation) / Part 1 (Dividends received in tax year):  
- A = ETF fund name (e.g. "ISHARES S&P/TSX COMPOSITE HIGH DIVIDEND INDEX ETF")  
- Type of Column F = s.112  
- F = G = eligible dividend amount (Box 49)  

---
Note that the S3 for T2 corporate tax filing is not to be confused with the Schedule 3 for personal tax.  
Here is the Schedule 3 which is relevant to corporations: https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t2sch3.html  

Canada follows the "tax integration" principle, where eligible dividends received by a corporation and paid out to shareholders are taxed similarly to the person receiving the dividends directly.  
To achieve this (avoid double-taxation), your T2 software automatically does the following:  
- Deduct taxable dividends under section 112 on line 320 of the T2
- Refundable taxes are tracked in two separate Refundable Dividend Tax on Hand (RDTOH) accounts based on the type of income earned (this is part of "tax integration" to avoid paying "extra" tax)  
  - Non-Eligible Refundable Dividend Tax on Hand (NERDTOH) tracks "extra" refundable tax paid on investment income that generally results in ineligible dividends
    Source: Part I Tax (the refundable portion) on interest, foreign income, and taxable capital gains  
    Source: Part IV Tax on non-eligible dividends received from other corporations
  - Eligible Refundable Dividend Tax on Hand (ERDTOH) tracks refundable tax on income that can be paid out as eligible dividends
    Source: Part IV Tax on eligible portfolio dividends received
    Source: Part IV Tax on dividends from "connected" corporations that triggered a refund from their own ERDTOH
- General Rate Income Pool (GRIP): separate account that tracks the capacity to pay eligible dividends, it's primarily increased by income taxed at the general corporate rate (rather than the small business rate) and by receiving eligible dividends from other companies  
- You get an NERDTOH refund by paying a non-eligible dividend (issue a T5 slip via CRA My Business Account WebForms)  
- You get an ERDTOH refund by paying an eligible dividend (which requires a specific election and a check of your GRIP balance); or by paying a non-eligible dividend if the NERDTOH is already empty  
- T2 / Page 7: Refundable dividend tax on hand  
- S3 / Page 3 / Part 3 / Box 450 = Total taxable dividends paid in the taxation year to other than connected corporations  
- S3 / Page 3 / Part 4 / Box 500 = "Total dividend paid in the tax year" (up to 450)  
Link: https://greenardgroup.com/refund-of-corporate-taxes-previously-paid/  


# Other income - Box 26  

Debit: "Deposits - investment" (1002-2) within year, or "Dividends receivable" (1060) if declared in December but paid in Jan.  
Credit: "Other investment income" (8094-1)  

Schedule 7 (S7) / Part 1 / line 032 - "Total income from property (including income from a specified investment business carried on in Canada other than income from a source outside Canada)"  
^^ depending on your tax software (I use FutureTax T2), you can double-click this box to get the following:  
S7WS - Schedule 7 Worksheet / "Other interest and property income"  
Enter 'T3 Box 26' (label) → Aggregate (column) = Box 26 amount  

The above Schedule 7 entry is necessary for the T2 software to apply the correct passive-income treatment (increase tax rate to ~50%, which is higher than the small business active income rate of 12.2% in Ontario).  
Investment income does not qualify for the Small Business Deduction (SBD), passive "Other income" is not tax-efficient in a corporation.  


# Foreign income - Box 25, and Foreign tax withheld - Box 34  

Debit Box 25 amount - Box 34 amount (net): "Deposits - investment" (1002-2) within year, or "Dividends receivable" (1060) if declared in December but paid in Jan.  
Debit Box 34 amount: "Withholding taxes" (9283)  
Credit Box 25 amount: "Dividends from foreign sources" (8097)  

Schedule 7 (S7) / Part 3 / Box 019 - Total income from property from a source outside Canada (net of related expenses)  
^^ depending on your tax software (I use FutureTax T2), you can double-click this box to get the following:  
S7WS - Schedule 7 Worksheet / Page 1 / Income from property ... Foreign (column) / Other dividends  
Enter the Box 25 amount, note that even though the section is called "net of related expenses", the full gross amount is entered (expenses handled separately).  

Schedule 21 / Part 1 - Federal foreign non-business income tax credit  
Avoid double taxation by filling out to deduct the foreign tax paid from your Canadian tax otherwise payable  

Schedule 1 / Page 3 / Other additions (Description 705; Amount 295)  
We are counting the foreign tax withheld as an expense in "Withholding taxes" (9283), and also we are claiming a credit in S21.  
This is double-dipping, and to avoid it enter the box 34 amount as "Foreign tax add-back" to "Other additions".  

If you don't enter either "Schedule 21 / Part 1" or "Schedule 1 / Page 3 / Other additions", then the foreign tax will be a regular expense, which is less tax-efficient.  

The above is true when withholding tax is expensed in the income statement, there is an alternative that some find simpler:  
- Debit the net amount only ("Deposits - investment"), without expensing "Withholding taxes"  
- Add withholding tax in S1 "Other additions" to report gross amount  


# Amount resulting in cost base adjustment, typically Return of Capital (ROC) - box 42

This is the fund giving you back your own money, the investment value is reduced by the amount of cash that you receive, so this is not a "real" distribution.  

Debit: "Deposits - investment" (1002-2) within year, or "Dividends receivable" (1060) if declared in December but paid in Jan.  
Credit: "Brokerage" (2303-1), this reduces the value of your investment by the corresponding amount.  

There is no specific Schedule or anything that needs to be specified, the effect of the ROC is an increased capital gain when selling.  
Note that the ACB cannot be negative, so if ROC would reduce it below zero then the negative amount instead becomes an immediate capital gain.  

When you retroactively classify a portion of the distribution as ROC, it involves reducing the value of your investment account, so the book cost will no longer match what was in your historical statements.
Your brokerage will not immediately reflect the ROC amount in Book Cost, and depending on the brokerage you may see the amount reflected in April (as shown in example below).
In order for your "Brokerage" (2303-1) account to match what you see in your brokerage statements and website, you can add a temporary entry for the first date of the following year (e.g. Jan 1st):  
Debit: "Brokerage" (2303-1)  
Credit: "Brokerage - pending" (2303-2)  
Amount: sum of all ROC distributions  
Description: ROC pending brokerage update in April - to be reversed  

And then in April when your brokerage finally reflects this, you can reverse the above:  
Debit: "Brokerage - pending" (2303-2)  
Credit: "Brokerage" (2303-1)  
Amount: sum of all ROC distributions  
Description: ROC pending brokerage update in April - reversal  


# Capital gains distribution - Box 21

Box 21 comes in two flavours:
- Cash: which appears in your "Deposits - investment" (1002-2) account  
- Phantom: non-cash amount, which is counted as a capital gain and increases ACB  
- Both amounts may be lumped into a single Box 21, in which case you can work out the split by looking at what shows up in "Deposits - investment" vs the T3 box 21 amount (or reconcile with CDS, see link above)  

Debit for Box 21:
- Cash: "Deposits - investment" (1002-2), or "Dividends receivable" (1060) if declared in December but paid in Jan
- Phantom: "Brokerage" (2303-1)

Credit (in either case): "Capital gains distributions" (8211-2)  

When you enter a phantom capital gains distribution, it increases the value of your investment account, which causes the book cost to diverge from the end-of-year statement.  
Add a temporary entry for the first date of the following year (Jan 1st):  
Debit: "Brokerage - pending" (2303-2)  
Credit: "Brokerage" (2303-1)  
Description: phantom capital gains distribution pending brokerage update in April - to be reversed  

And then in April when your brokerage finally reflects this, reverse the above:
Debit: "Brokerage" (2303-1)  
Credit: "Brokerage - pending" (2303-2)  
Description: phantom capital gains distribution pending brokerage update in April - reversal  

In addition to the above ledger entries, enter the Box 21 amount in the T2:
- Schedule 6 (Summary of Dispositions of Capital Property) / Part 4 (Other properties)  
  "Capital gains distribution - ETF trust T3 Box 21"  
  Report the amount as a capital gain, half of it will be taxed instead of the full amount (based on capital gains inclusion rate)  
  Note that this is reported under "Dispositions" even though these are capital gains from a trust (you did not dispose of anything)  
- Schedule 1 (Net Income (Loss) for Income Tax Purposes) / Page 4 / Other deductions (Description 705 / Amount 395)  
  "Capital gains distribution T3 Box 21 - reported on Schedule 6"  
  Deduct non-taxable portion (50%) to reconcile financial statement treatment to tax treatment (since capital gains are taxed via Schedule 6)

Track the non-taxable portion (50%) of the Box 21 amount in the Capital Dividend Account (CDA, see Related below).
