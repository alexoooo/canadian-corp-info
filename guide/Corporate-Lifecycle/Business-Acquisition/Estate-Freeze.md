# Estate Freeze

See parent document: [Business Acquisition](Business-Acquisition.md)

**Who this is for**:
- Owner of a CCPC transferring a business to the next generation, or a family member buying the business from a parent
- Want to understand how a freeze fixes today's value, funds retirement, and interacts with anti-avoidance rules

**TLDR**:
- A *freeze* swaps the current owner's growth shares for fixed-value preferred shares
  - New common shares are issued to the next generation
- Future growth accrues to the new common; the parent's value is locked into the preferred shares
- The corporation redeems those preferred shares over time to fund the parent's retirement
  - Each redemption is a deemed dividend
- ITA *s.84.1* blocks turning that payout into a capital gain in a family deal
  - A relieving exception covers genuine intergenerational transfers

Limitations:
- Orientation only; a freeze is set up by professionals, with a valuation and usually a price-adjustment clause
- The intergenerational-transfer conditions are summarized
  - The statute is detailed and the conditions must be met before and after closing
- Tax figures are 2026 amounts and change; verify before relying on them
- The following is my understanding as of 2026


## Fixing Value and Shifting Growth

An *estate freeze* fixes the current owner's stake at today's value.  
It shifts future growth to someone else, usually children or a family trust.  

The steps, in outline:
- The owner exchanges their common (growth) shares for *fixed-value, redeemable, retractable preferred shares*
  - Their redemption value equals the business's current worth
- New *common shares* are issued, for nominal value, to the next generation
- From that point, the preferred shares stay fixed and all future growth accrues to the new common shares

Two results follow:
- The owner's eventual capital gain is *frozen* at today's value
  - On death, the deemed disposition at fair market value (ITA s.70(5)) is capped at the frozen preferred-share value
  - Without the freeze, that value would keep rising with the business
- The growth, and its future tax, passes to the next generation from the moment of the freeze


## The Mechanics

A freeze is carried out with the same rollover machinery as a share-funded purchase.  
The gain is deferred on the exchange:
- *Section 86*: a reorganization of capital, exchanging the common shares for new preferred shares of the *same* corporation
  - No election form is required (ITA [s.86](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-86.html))
- *Section 85*: a rollover of the shares to a *new* holding corporation in exchange for preferred shares
  - On a joint election (Form T2057), where a holdco is wanted in the structure

The preferred shares take a low paid-up capital, and their fixed redemption value is set by a *valuation* of the business.  
The PUC grind caps that paid-up capital at the old shares' PUC less any boot.  
ITA [s.86(2.1)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-86.html) applies on the s.86 route, [s.85(2.1)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-85.html) on the s.85 route.  
A *price-adjustment clause* is normal, so the value can be corrected if CRA later disputes it.  


## Freezing at Incorporation

The routes above assume the business already sits inside a corporation.  
Where it is still a *sole proprietorship* or *partnership*, there are no shares to exchange.  
The freeze is instead built into the incorporation itself:
- *Section 86* and the *section 85* roll to a holding corporation both exchange existing shares
  - An unincorporated business has none, so neither is available at this stage
- The freeze instead rides on the *section 85 rollover* that incorporates the business

The mechanics, on incorporation:
- The proprietor (or the partners) transfers the business *assets* to a new corporation under ITA [s.85(1)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-85.html)
  - At *elected amounts* that defer the accrued gains on goodwill (Class 14.1), depreciable property, and inventory
  - Receivables usually pass at face with a joint s.22 election, which preserves the corporation's bad-debt deductions
- In place of growth shares, the transferor takes back *fixed-value, redeemable, retractable preferred shares*
  - Their redemption value equals the current worth of what was rolled in
- The *common (growth) shares* are subscribed for nominal value by the next generation, directly or through a family trust
- Future growth accrues to the new common and the transferor's value is capped in the preferred shares
  - The same result as a freeze inside an existing corporation, reached at the moment of incorporation

For a *partnership*, each partner can transfer their partnership interest under s.85(1).  
Or the partnership transfers its property under ITA [s.85(2)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-85.html) and is wound up under [s.85(3)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-85.html).  
Each partner decides independently whether to freeze their own share.  

Three points differ from the existing-corporation case:
- *s.84.1 is not engaged at this step*: that rule applies to a transfer of *shares* to a non-arm's-length corporation
  - The incorporation freeze transfers *assets*
  - s.84.1 and its intergenerational exception below bear only on a later transfer of the freeze shares
- *The lifetime capital gains exemption is available but not automatic*
  - The 24-month QSBC holding period is no bar here
    - ITA [s.110.6(14)(f)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-110.6.html) relieves it for shares issued on the roll-in of all or substantially all of an active business
    - So the shares can qualify from issuance despite the business's earlier life as a proprietorship
  - Using the exemption still takes a deliberate *crystallization* that triggers a capital gain on the shares
    - A reorganization or election, since incorporating alone does not use it
  - The corporation must meet the small-business-corporation asset tests
- *Active business vs property*: the mechanics work for any asset, but a passive property fares worse
  - A passive rental or investment property earns no QSBC or lifetime-exemption treatment
  - It gives up the principal-residence exemption (available only to individuals, not corporations)
  - It faces the higher (partly refundable) tax on passive investment income
  - The caveat weighs far more heavily for a property than for an operating business

One point is specific to the freeze rather than to a plain incorporation.  
It is the transferor's *freeze preferred shares* that must be QSBC shares to carry the exemption.  
Any gain the s.85 roll-in is elected to trigger falls on the transferred *assets*, not on those shares.  
Asset-level gains here mean goodwill and recapture on depreciable property.  
The exemption shelters only a gain on the shares, so it does not reach that asset-level gain.  
Crystallizing the LCGE therefore takes a further, deliberate step.  
Typically a later s.85 or s.86 share exchange engineered to realize a gain up to the available exemption.  
Whether to crystallize at all, rather than leave the exemption for a future sale, is itself a planning choice.  


## Funding Retirement by Redeeming the Freeze Shares

The freeze does not by itself put cash in the parent's hands; the preferred shares do that as they are redeemed.  
The corporation redeems the freeze preferred shares over time, out of retained earnings.  
The basis is the same as any share-funded purchase (see [Preferred-Share Consideration](Preferred-Share-Consideration.md)).  
Each redemption above paid-up capital is a deemed dividend under ITA [s.84(3)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-84.html).  

This is how "paying the previous owner" works in the family case.  
A stream of redemptions funds the parent's retirement, spread over years.  


## Section 84.1 and Surplus Stripping

ITA [s.84.1](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-84.1.html) is the anti-avoidance rule that governs the family case.  
It applies when an *individual* transfers shares of one corporation to another they do not deal with at arm's length.  
It stops the individual from converting what should be a dividend into a more lightly taxed capital gain.  

Without s.84.1, a parent could sell shares to a corporation owned by their child and take back a note or boosted PUC.  
They would claim a capital gain (and the lifetime capital gains exemption) on surplus that is just retained earnings.  
s.84.1 blocks this by grinding the PUC and/or deeming a dividend on the transfer.  


## Intergenerational-Transfer Exception

A genuine transfer of a family business to the next generation is excepted from s.84.1.  
The parent keeps capital-gains treatment and the lifetime capital gains exemption.  
The exception is in ITA s.84.1(2)(e), introduced by Bill C-208.  
Budget 2023 tightened it for transfers on or after January 1, 2024.  

The transfer must follow one of two pathways:
- *Immediate transfer*: both legal and factual control pass to the child's corporation
  - The balance of shares is transferred within three years
  - The child must retain control and stay actively involved for at least three years after the transfer
- *Gradual transfer*: legal control passes immediately and the parent's equity is significantly reduced within ten years
  - The child must retain control and stay actively involved for at least five years after the transfer
    - Or until the transfer is complete, if later

A further condition: the parent can use the exception only once for shares deriving their value from the same business.  
Both pathways also require the parent and child(ren) to jointly elect in prescribed form.  
The election is filed on or before the parent's filing-due date for the year of the disposition.  
ITA [s.84.1(2.31)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-84.1.html) covers the immediate transfer, [s.84.1(2.32)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-84.1.html) the gradual one.  
Without the election the exception is unavailable, however genuine the transfer.  
The conditions are strict and tested after closing, so this is firmly advisor territory.  


## Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption

Where the exception applies, the parent can shelter the capital gain with the *lifetime capital gains exemption* (LCGE).  
The shares must be *qualified small business corporation* (QSBC) shares.  
The LCGE is up to $1,275,000 for 2026 (ITA [s.110.6](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-110.6.html)).  
The capital gains inclusion rate is one-half for 2026 (the proposed increase to two-thirds was cancelled in 2025).  

A freeze is often timed to *multiply* the exemption across family members (or beneficiaries of a family trust).  
This multiplication is one of the freeze's planning attractions.  


## Corporate Attribution Trap

A freeze benefiting a *designated person* can trigger corporate attribution under ITA [s.74.4(2)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-74.4.html).  
A designated person is the freezor's spouse, or a trust with minor beneficiaries.  
The freezor is taxed on deemed interest at the prescribed rate on the value frozen.  
The deemed amount is reduced by dividends actually paid out.  
The rule applies only while the corporation is *not* a *small business corporation* (s.74.4(2)(c)).  
It mainly bites where the frozen corporation holds investment assets rather than running an active business.  
A freeze whose growth accrues to *adult* children is not caught, since they are not designated persons (ITA [s.74.5(5)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-74.5.html)).  
This is one more reason a freeze involving a spouse or minor beneficiaries is advisor territory.  
Check it alongside TOSI (s.120.4) on any dividends the next generation receives.
