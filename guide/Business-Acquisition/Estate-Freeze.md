# Estate Freeze

See parent document: [Business Acquisition](Business-Acquisition.md)

**Who this is for**:
- Owner of a CCPC transferring a business to the next generation, or a family member buying the business from a parent
- Want to understand how a freeze fixes today's value, funds the parent's retirement, and interacts with the anti-avoidance rules

**TLDR**:
- A *freeze* swaps the current owner's growth shares for fixed-value preferred shares, and issues new common shares to the next generation
- Future growth accrues to the new common; the parent's value is locked into the preferred shares
- The corporation redeems those preferred shares over time to fund the parent's retirement, each redemption a deemed dividend
- ITA *s.84.1* blocks turning that payout into a capital gain in a family deal, with a relieving exception for genuine intergenerational transfers

- Orientation only; a freeze is set up by professionals, with a valuation and usually a price-adjustment clause
- The intergenerational-transfer conditions are summarized; the statute is detailed and the conditions must be met before and after closing

## What a freeze does

An *estate freeze* fixes the current owner's stake at today's value and shifts future growth to someone else, usually children or a family trust.  

The steps, in outline:
- The owner exchanges their common (growth) shares for *fixed-value, redeemable, retractable preferred shares* with a redemption value equal to the business's current worth
- New *common shares* are issued, for nominal value, to the next generation
- From that point, the preferred shares stay fixed and all future growth accrues to the new common shares

Two results follow:
- The owner's eventual capital gain is *frozen* at today's value; on death, the deemed disposition of their shares at fair market value (ITA s.70(5)) is capped at the frozen preferred-share value rather than rising with the business
- The growth, and its future tax, passes to the next generation from the moment of the freeze


## The mechanics

A freeze is carried out with the same rollover machinery as a share-funded purchase, so the gain is deferred on the exchange:
- *Section 86*: a reorganization of capital, exchanging the old common shares for new preferred shares of the *same* corporation; no election form is required (ITA [s.86](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-86.html))
- *Section 85*: a rollover of the shares to a *new* holding corporation in exchange for preferred shares, on a joint election (Form T2057), where a holdco is wanted in the structure

The preferred shares take a low paid-up capital, and their fixed redemption value is set by a *valuation* of the business.  
A *price-adjustment clause* is normal, so the value can be corrected if CRA later disputes it.  


## Funding retirement by redeeming the freeze shares

The freeze does not by itself put cash in the parent's hands; the preferred shares do that as they are redeemed.  
The corporation redeems the freeze preferred shares over time, out of retained earnings, on the same basis as any share-funded purchase: each redemption above paid-up capital is a deemed dividend under ITA [s.84(3)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-84.html) (see [Preferred-share consideration](Preferred-Share-Consideration.md)).  

This is how "paying the previous owner" works in the family case: a stream of redemptions funds the parent's retirement, spread over years.  


## Section 84.1 and surplus stripping

ITA [s.84.1](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-84.1.html) is the anti-avoidance rule that governs the family case.  
It applies when an *individual* transfers shares of one corporation to another corporation they do not deal with at arm's length, and it stops the individual from converting what should be a dividend into a more lightly taxed capital gain.  

Without s.84.1, a parent could sell their shares to a corporation owned by their child, take back a note or boosted PUC, and claim a capital gain (and the lifetime capital gains exemption) on surplus that is just the company's retained earnings.  
s.84.1 blocks this by grinding the PUC and/or deeming a dividend on the transfer.  


## Intergenerational-transfer exception

A genuine transfer of a family business to the next generation is excepted from s.84.1, so the parent can keep capital-gains treatment and the lifetime capital gains exemption.  
The exception is in ITA s.84.1(2)(e), introduced by Bill C-208 and tightened by Budget 2023 for transfers on or after January 1, 2024.  

The transfer must follow one of two pathways:
- *Immediate transfer*: both legal and factual control pass to the child's corporation, with the balance of shares transferred within three years; the child must retain control and stay actively involved for at least three years after the transfer
- *Gradual transfer*: legal control passes immediately and the parent's equity is significantly reduced within ten years; the child must retain control and stay involved for the later of five years or completion of the transfer

A further condition: the parent can use the exception only once for shares deriving their value from the same business.  
The conditions are strict and tested after closing, so this is firmly advisor territory.  


## Lifetime capital gains exemption

Where the exception applies and the shares are *qualified small business corporation* (QSBC) shares, the parent can shelter the capital gain with the *lifetime capital gains exemption* (LCGE), up to $1,275,000 for 2026 (ITA [s.110.6](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-3.3/section-110.6.html)).  
The capital gains inclusion rate is one-half for 2026 (the proposed increase to two-thirds was cancelled in 2025).  

A freeze is often timed to *multiply* the exemption across family members holding shares (or beneficiaries of a family trust), which is one of its planning attractions.
