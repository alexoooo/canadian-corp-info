# Incorporation vs Sole Proprietorship

**Who this is for**:
- Self-employed contractors deciding whether to incorporate or keep operating personally
- CCPC owners revisiting whether the structure still earns its keep

**TLDR**:
- Integration makes the headline tax roughly a wash on income you spend; the decision is made by the secondary lines:
  - Deferral: profit left in the corp is taxed at the small-business rate instead of your marginal rate
    - It only runs on money you do not spend
  - PSB risk: a one-client incorporated contractor risks *worse*-than-sole-prop taxation
  - CPP: mandatory both halves as a sole prop; avoidable (dividends-only) in a corp
  - Losses: sole-prop losses offset your other income now; corporate losses stay in the corp
- Client requirements and liability often decide the question by themselves
- The administrative load is real: the corporate apparatus this whole guide documents, against one T2125

Limitations:
- Ontario rates where figures appear; they are 2026 and structural, not a substitute for running the year's numbers
- The incorporation *mechanics* are [Starting Up](../Corporate-Lifecycle/Starting-Up.md)'s territory; this page is the whether
- Partnerships, professional corporations, and holdco structures are out of scope
- The following is my understanding as of 2026


## The Same Profit Two Ways

$100,000 of consulting profit, Ontario:

| | Sole proprietor | CCPC |
|---|---|---|
| Taxpayers | one: you (T1) | two: the corp (T2) plus you (T1) |
| Tax this year | your marginal rates on the full profit | ≈ 12.2% now; personal tax only on what you take out |
| The money you live on | already yours, after tax | extracted as salary or dividends, taxed then |
| CPP | both halves, mandatory (Schedule 8) | salary: both halves; dividends: none |
| RRSP room | accrues on the earned income | salary yes, dividends no |
| A loss year | deducts against your other income | stays in the corp as a carryover |
| Slip from clients | T4A box 048 | generally none |
| GST/HST | same regime, `RT` account under your BN | same regime, corp BN |
| Filings | T2125 inside the T1, June 15 | T2, registry return, payroll or T5 season, separate books |
| Liability | personal, unlimited | limited, with caveats |

On income earned and paid out in the same year, integration makes the two columns' combined tax land close.  
The arithmetic is worked on [Tax Integration](../Overview/Tax-Integration.md).  
The corporate advantage is not the headline rate; it is what the structure makes possible.  
What follows is the lines that actually differ.  


## Deferral Is the Engine

- The gap: roughly 12.2% small-business rate against personal marginal rates reaching ~53.5% at the Ontario top bracket
- The gap is deferral, not forgiveness: personal tax comes due when the money comes out
- It compounds only on profit left in the corp
  - A contractor who spends everything earned gets no deferral, only the structure's costs
- Retained profit invested corporately brings its own regime: the AAII grind and refundable tax
  - See [Small Business Tax Overview](../Overview/Small-Business-Tax.md#active-vs-investment-income) and [Investments](../Investments/Investments.md)


## The PSB Trap

The same facts that make an unincorporated worker an employee make a CCPC a *Personal Service Business*.  
PSB taxation is worse than staying sole: no SBD, the base rate plus 5%, and almost no deductions (s.18(1)(p)).  
The factor test and consequences are worked in [Small Business Tax Overview](../Overview/Small-Business-Tax.md#personal-service-business-classification-risk).  

- As a sole prop, one-client dependence is only the employee-vs-contractor question (see [Becoming a Sole Proprietor](Becoming-A-Sole-Proprietor.md#employee-or-contractor))
- Incorporating the same facts raises the stakes: the reclassification penalty is now the PSB regime
- Where the client requires a corporation anyway, manage the factors deliberately
  - Multiple clients where possible, own tools and hours, own insurance, a documented independent relationship


## CPP: Mandatory vs Optional

- A sole prop cannot opt out: both halves follow the net income (see [CPP and the T1](CPP-And-The-T1.md))
- A corp owner chooses: salary triggers both halves, dividends none
  - Skipping CPP is a retirement-planning decision, not a tax rounding; the trade is worked in [Salary vs Dividends](../Paying-Yourself/Salary-Vs-Dividends.md#cpp-cost-now-entitlement-later)


## Losses in the Early Years

- A sole-prop business loss deducts against your other income (employment, investments) in the same year
- A corporate loss stays in the corp: back 3 years, forward 20, against corporate income only (see [Losses](../Filing-And-CRA/Losses.md))
- A venture expected to lose money at first, beside other income, argues for staying unincorporated until profitable


## Liability, Insurance, and What Clients Demand

- Limited liability is real but caveated
  - Banks and landlords want personal guarantees from a small corp
  - Professional negligence follows the person; insurance is needed under either structure
- Many agencies and enterprise clients contract only with corporations
  - For IT and consulting contractors this requirement often decides the question by itself
- Regulated professions have their own professional-corporation regimes, out of scope here


## LCGE on Exit

- A business with sale value held in a corporation can grow into QSBC shares
  - A share sale can then claim the *lifetime capital gains exemption* (ITA s.110.6; see [Asset vs Share Purchase](../Corporate-Lifecycle/Business-Acquisition/Asset-vs-Share.md))
- A sole proprietorship exits by asset sale on personal account, with no LCGE
- The QSBC tests want lead time
  - Incorporating an existing proprietorship gets relief from the 24-month holding test (s.110.6(14)(f))
- A pure consulting practice with no saleable goodwill weighs this line at zero


## Administrative Load and Cost

- Sole prop: the books, one T2125, and possibly an `RT` account
- Corp: incorporation, registry returns, separate books and bank accounts, the T2, payroll or dividend-slip seasons
  - The apparatus is what the rest of this guide documents
- The annual accounting bill is a real recurring number; budget for it before counting the deferral savings


## Cases Decided Outright

- *You spend everything you earn and have one client*: stay sole
  - There is no deferral to harvest, and incorporating maximizes PSB risk
- *Profit reliably exceeds spending by a wide margin*: incorporate; the deferral compounds
- *The client or agency requires a corporation*: incorporate, and manage the PSB factors deliberately
- *Early losses beside other income*: stay sole until profitable
- *A sale of the business is plausible*: incorporate with QSBC lead time in mind
- *Liability-heavy work*: incorporate, and still carry insurance

The common path is sequential: start sole, incorporate once deferral or a client demand makes the apparatus worth it.  


## Making the Move

- The sole-prop business ceases at the transfer; its final T2125 covers January 1 to the transfer date
- Roll the going business (equipment, goodwill, client list) into the corp under ITA s.85
  - Worked at orientation level in [Starting Up - Bringing in Assets](../Corporate-Lifecycle/Starting-Up.md#bringing-in-assets); professional-advice territory
- GST/HST on the transfer can be relieved by a joint ETA s.167 election
  - It requires substantially all the property needed to carry on the business
  - The buyer must be a registrant where the seller is one
  - Register the corp's `RT` account before the transfer, then close your own
- The corp starts its own clocks: BN, small-supplier test, first-year T2 (see [Starting Up](../Corporate-Lifecycle/Starting-Up.md#first-year-clocks))
